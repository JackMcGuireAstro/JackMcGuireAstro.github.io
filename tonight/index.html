Tonight’s sky

Pick an observatory and a night. This page works out when it gets dark and which targets from CTAS or WorldsIndex stay above your altitude limit, for how long, and how far each one is from the Moon, or which known exoplanets transit their star while you can watch. Everything is computed in your browser from this site’s published catalogs. Nothing you enter is sent anywhere.

How this is worked out

The Sun and Moon come from low-precision almanac formulae, accurate to about 0.01° for the Sun and 0.3° for the Moon. That is plenty for planning, but it is not good enough to point a telescope. The night is sampled every five minutes from local noon to the next noon. “Hours up” counts the dark samples with the target above your altitude limit. Airmass uses the Kasten & Young (1989) formula. Atmospheric refraction, the horizon profile and telescope limits are ignored.

CTAS transients are the current follow-up targets in the live CTAS release. Their brightness is the reported discovery magnitude, so it is not a forecast. WorldsIndex systems are grouped by host star and include confirmed planets by default. Check a target’s dossier before you spend telescope time on it.

Transit predictions

Each planet’s period, mid-transit epoch, duration and depth come from one row of a NASA Exoplanet Archive table, chosen by WorldsIndex: the PSCompPars row if it gives an epoch, else the Planetary Systems default row, else the most recent Planetary Systems row, else the TESS, K2 or Kepler table row whose prediction for today is most precise. Values are never mixed between rows. The transit list lives in the WorldsIndex release, and each row links to the planet’s page there.

A transit is listed when part of it, from ingress to egress, falls in the dark hours with the star above your altitude limit. “Full” means all of it does; “Partial” gives the share that does. The timing uncertainty grows with every orbit since the catalog epoch: σ = √(σT0² + (n·σP)²), where n is the number of orbits. When a catalog gives different upper and lower errors, the larger one is used. Old or loose ephemerides can be hours off, so the uncertainty filter hides those by default.

Catalog epochs are in Barycentric Julian Date on the TDB time scale (BJDTDB): the moment the light would reach the centre of mass of the solar system. Earth is up to 8.3 light-minutes from that point, so a transit seen from Earth can come up to about 8 minutes earlier or later than its BJD time, depending on the season and where the star is in the sky, and TDB runs 69 seconds ahead of UTC. This page converts each predicted time with Earth’s position from the same solar formulae used for the Sun (plus Jupiter’s and Saturn’s pull on the Sun) and the 37 leap seconds in force since 2017. In tests against astropy it agrees to within a few seconds, well inside any catalog’s timing uncertainty. Ingress and egress are mid-time ± half the catalog duration. Allow extra time before and after for a baseline.
