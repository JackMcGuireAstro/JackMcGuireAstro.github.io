Research

Stars that did not evolve alone

Most of what I work on comes back to one question: when two stars interact, what evidence survives, and how long can we still read it? The answers show up in surface chemistry, in orbital architecture, and in radii we can measure directly.

Primary research program

Barium Dwarfs and Post-AGB Mass Transfer

The question
Barium dwarfs are main-sequence stars whose surfaces carry heavy elements they could never have made themselves. The elements came from a companion that has since become a white dwarf. Known barium dwarfs cluster strongly among F- and G-type stars, so where are the cooler K- and M-type ones?
Why it matters
If cool barium dwarfs are genuinely rare, that tells us something physical about how efficiently an AGB star can pollute a low-mass companion, and at what separations. If instead they are simply harder to find, then the census that underpins a lot of binary-evolution work is biased in a way worth correcting.
My role
I led the sample analysis under Dr. Russel J. White at Georgia State University, first as a Raghavan Fellow and then as an undergraduate research assistant, and I am first author on the manuscript in preparation.
Data and methods
I investigated the 71 spectroscopically confirmed barium dwarfs using Gaia DR3 astrometry, radial-velocity statistics, archival spectroscopy, spectral-energy-distribution fitting with VOSA, and MIST evolutionary models. Python workflows built on NumPy, Pandas, Astropy, Astroquery and Matplotlib cross-match the catalogs, separate genuine dwarfs from evolved interlopers, and compare the sample against known white-dwarf–main-sequence binaries.
Where it stands
The work has moved well past its November 2025 poster. Establishing the F/G concentration motivated a targeted search for the missing cool population, and that search, together with comparisons to established white-dwarf systems and upper and lower bounds on companion masses forms the manuscript Where Are the K & M Barium Dwarfs?, in preparation with planned submission to an American Astronomical Society journal in Fall 2026.

Focused investigation

GJ 166 C and the 40 Eridani System

The question
40 Eridani is a nearby triple containing a white dwarf and a low-mass companion, and on paper, an ideal place to catch a cool barium dwarf in the act. Does GJ 166 C actually show the s-process enrichment that post-AGB mass transfer would leave behind?
Why it matters
A single well-characterised nearby system constrains the geometry of the problem far more sharply than a statistical sample can. Whatever 40 Eridani shows, it applies directly to the broader question of where the cool barium dwarfs went.
My role
I carried out the analysis and collaborated with Dr. Richard O. Gray of Appalachian State University on the high-resolution spectroscopic search.
Data and methods
High-resolution spectroscopy targeting s-process abundance signatures, interpreted alongside the system's known orbital parameters and the CHARA interferometric work described below.
Where it stands
The search returned a null result: no detectable s-process enrichment. Read together with the system's roughly 200-year orbit, that null result constrains the separation at which AGB mass transfer can still work efficiently, which is a genuinely useful constraint, and it feeds directly into the wider barium-dwarf study. It has not been published separately.

Observational program

CHARA Interferometry and Low-Mass Stars

The question
Low-mass stars are persistently observed to be larger than stellar-evolution models predict. Optical interferometry is one of the very few ways to measure a stellar diameter directly rather than inferring it, so it can test that discrepancy head-on.
Why it matters
M dwarfs are the most common stars in the galaxy and host a large share of known planets. If our radii for them are systematically wrong, so are the planet radii, densities and irradiation levels derived from them.
My role
I planned and led two half-night observing sessions with PI Becky Flores on the six-telescope CHARA Array in November 2025, and reduced and modelled the resulting and archival data.
Data and methods
Observations with MIRC-X in H band and MYSTIC in K band, targeting nearby M dwarfs in binary systems. The work covers fringe locking and visibility calibration through to uniform-disk and limb-darkened model fits, uncertainty propagation, and cross-checks between independent reduction pipelines. Four nights of MIRC-X and MYSTIC data were reduced in total.
Where it stands
Modelling of archival GJ 166 C observations indicates an approximately 30 percent radius excess relative to stellar-evolution model predictions. Analysis is ongoing, and results are contributing to a manuscript on M-dwarf radius inflation.

Instrumentation and space weather

Cosmic Rays and Space Weather

The question
Secondary cosmic rays reaching the ground carry an imprint of both the atmosphere they passed through and the solar activity that modulated them upstream. Can a network of inexpensive detectors separate those effects well enough to be scientifically useful?
Why it matters
Ground-level muon monitoring is a cheap, distributed way to watch space weather. It also makes an unusually good outreach instrument, because the physics is visible in real time.
My role
Cosmic-ray research assistant at Georgia State University with Dr. Xiaochun He and Dr. Viacheslav Sadykov: detector assembly, data analysis, and outreach.
Data and methods
I assembled Raspberry Pi-based detectors for the gLOWCOST global muon-monitoring network, then analysed multi-year muon-flux measurements against atmospheric data and solar and lunar ephemerides using Fourier transforms, coherence analysis and correlation tests to look for environmental and tidal modulation. Separately, I studied Forbush decreases by comparing gLOWCOST measurements with ACE MAG and SWEPAM solar-wind and magnetic-field data from the L1 point.
Where it stands
Ongoing analysis; the detector-based outreach work was also part of what I presented at the Georgia State Capitol in February 2026.

Astronomical data analysis · coursework project

Mapping the Magellanic Clouds

The question
How accurately can the distances and line-of-sight structure of the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds be recovered from public survey catalogs alone?
Why it matters
The Clouds anchor the local distance ladder, and standard candles within them are the practical test of whether a period–luminosity calibration behaves.
My role
Sole author of the analysis, carried out for ASTR 4200 in Fall 2025.
Data and methods
OGLE variable-star catalogs combined with 2MASS near-infrared photometry and Gaia astrometry. Proper-motion filtering isolates genuine members; Cepheid and RR Lyrae period–luminosity relations then give distances, and the distribution of those distances traces the structure along the line of sight.
Where it stands
Complete as a data-analysis project, presented as both a talk and a poster. This is coursework and a demonstration of catalog-level analysis, not a refereed research result.

Refereed publication

797 Montana

The question
What is the rotation period and light-curve amplitude of the main-belt asteroid 797 Montana, and do they agree with earlier measurements?
My role
Co-author. I took part in the observing campaign and the photometric reduction as part of the Spring 2025 astronomical techniques and instrumentation course at Georgia State University.
Data and methods
A four-night photometric campaign in February 2025 using the 24-inch Miller telescope at Hard Labor Creek Observatory, with CCD photometry reduced in IRAF and DS9.
Result
A best-fit rotation period of 4.545 ± 0.001 hr, consistent with previous measurements, with a variability amplitude of ΔV = 0.50 ± 0.03 mag. Published in the Minor Planet Bulletin.
