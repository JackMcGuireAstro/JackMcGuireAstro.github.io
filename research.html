Primary research program

Barium Dwarfs and Post-AGB Mass Transfer

Research map ↑

What does the scarcity of cool barium dwarfs tell us about binary evolution?

Recorded status
Manuscript in preparation
Publication state
Not yet submitted in the source summary
Activity dates
2025–2026 reports and posters; full project dates not recorded
Last research update
Date not recorded

The current objective is to separate selection effects from physical limits on AGB mass transfer.

The linked report and posters document earlier stages of the work; they do not establish completion or acceptance of the manuscript.

Available work April 2026 poster (PDF) · 2025 research report (PDF)

Question, contribution & methods +
The question
Barium dwarfs are main-sequence stars whose surfaces carry heavy elements they could never have made themselves. The elements came from a companion that has since become a white dwarf. Known barium dwarfs cluster strongly among F- and G-type stars, so where are the cooler K- and M-type ones?
Why it matters
If cool barium dwarfs are genuinely rare, that tells us something physical about how efficiently an AGB star can pollute a low-mass companion, and at what separations. If instead they are simply harder to find, then the census that underpins a lot of binary-evolution work is biased in a way worth correcting.
My role
I led the sample analysis under Dr. Russel J. White at Georgia State University, first as a Raghavan Fellow and then as an undergraduate research assistant, and I am first author on the manuscript in preparation.
Data and methods
The analysis described here investigated a sample of 71 spectroscopically confirmed barium dwarfs using Gaia DR3 astrometry, radial-velocity statistics, archival spectroscopy, spectral-energy-distribution fitting with VOSA, and MIST evolutionary models. Python workflows built on NumPy, Pandas, Astropy, Astroquery and Matplotlib cross-match the catalogs, separate genuine dwarfs from evolved interlopers, and compare the sample against known white-dwarf–main-sequence binaries.
Recorded status and interpretation
The work has moved well past its November 2025 poster. Establishing the F/G concentration motivated a targeted search for the missing cool population, and that search, together with comparisons to established white-dwarf systems and upper and lower bounds on companion masses forms part of a manuscript in preparation. The existing portfolio records a planned submission to an American Astronomical Society journal in Fall 2026; this is a plan, not a submission or acceptance.

Focused investigation

GJ 166 C and the 40 Eridani System

Research map ↑

Does the low-mass star beside the 40 Eridani white dwarf retain a chemical signature of mass transfer?

Recorded status
Null result reported
Publication state
Not published separately
Activity dates
Discussed at the August 7, 2025 symposium; full analysis dates not recorded
Last research update
Date not recorded

The reported search found no detectable s-process enrichment, providing a system-specific constraint for the wider barium-dwarf investigation.

This is an unpublished investigation. No detectable enrichment is a limit of the reported search, not proof that mass transfer never occurred.

Available work 2025 barium-dwarf report (PDF) · Symposium record

Question, contribution & methods +
The question
40 Eridani is a nearby triple containing a white dwarf and a low-mass companion, and on paper, an ideal place to catch a cool barium dwarf in the act. Does GJ 166 C actually show the s-process enrichment that post-AGB mass transfer would leave behind?
Why it matters
A single well-characterised nearby system constrains the geometry of the problem far more sharply than a statistical sample can. Whatever 40 Eridani shows, it applies directly to the broader question of where the cool barium dwarfs went.
My role
I carried out the analysis and collaborated with Dr. Richard O. Gray of Appalachian State University on the high-resolution spectroscopic search.
Data and methods
High-resolution spectroscopy targeting s-process abundance signatures, interpreted alongside the system's known orbital parameters and the CHARA interferometric work described below.
Recorded status and interpretation
The search returned a null result: no detectable s-process enrichment. Read together with the system's roughly 200-year orbit, that null result constrains the separation at which AGB mass transfer can still work efficiently, and feeds directly into the wider barium-dwarf study. It has not been published separately.

CHARA Interferometry and Low-Mass Stars

Research map ↑

Can directly measured stellar diameters explain why some M dwarfs are larger than models predict?

Recorded status
Ongoing analysis
Publication state
Contributing to a manuscript; preliminary result
Activity dates
November 2025 observing sessions; full analysis dates not recorded
Last research update
Date not recorded

Preliminary modelling of archival GJ 166 C observations indicates approximately 30% radius excess; the analysis remains ongoing.

The presentation explains the observing method. The radius-excess estimate is a preliminary portfolio result, not a separately linked refereed measurement.

Available work CHARA methods presentation (PDF) · Outreach record

Question, contribution & methods +
The question
Low-mass stars are persistently observed to be larger than stellar-evolution models predict. Optical interferometry is one of the very few ways to measure a stellar diameter directly rather than inferring it, so it can test that discrepancy head-on.
Why it matters
M dwarfs are the most common stars in the galaxy and host a large share of known planets. If our radii for them are systematically wrong, so are the planet radii, densities and irradiation levels derived from them.
My role
I planned and led two half-night observing sessions with PI Becky Flores on the six-telescope CHARA Array in November 2025, and reduced and modelled the resulting and archival data.
Data and methods
Observations with MIRC-X in H band and MYSTIC in K band, targeting nearby M dwarfs in binary systems. The work covers fringe locking and visibility calibration through to uniform-disk and limb-darkened model fits, uncertainty propagation, and cross-checks between independent reduction pipelines. Four nights of MIRC-X and MYSTIC data were reduced in total.
Recorded status and interpretation
Preliminary modelling of archival GJ 166 C observations indicates an approximately 30 percent radius excess relative to stellar-evolution model predictions. Analysis is ongoing, and results are contributing to a manuscript on M-dwarf radius inflation.

Cosmic Rays and Space Weather

Research map ↑

Can inexpensive muon detectors distinguish atmospheric variability from space-weather signals?

Recorded status
Analysis in progress in the source summary
Publication state
Presentation available; publication state not recorded
Activity dates
February 2026 outreach; full research dates not recorded
Last research update
Date not recorded

Detector assembly, multi-year time-series analysis, and public outreach connect ground-level measurements to their environmental context.

The linked presentation covers the analysis and outreach; no new quantitative space-weather result is claimed here.

Available work gLOWCOST presentation (PDF) · Capitol outreach record

Question, contribution & methods +
The question
Secondary cosmic rays reaching the ground carry an imprint of both the atmosphere they passed through and the solar activity that modulated them upstream. Can a network of inexpensive detectors separate those effects well enough to be scientifically useful?
Why it matters
Ground-level muon monitoring is a cheap, distributed way to watch space weather. It also makes an unusually good outreach instrument, because the physics is visible in real time.
My role
Cosmic-ray research assistant at Georgia State University with Dr. Xiaochun He and Dr. Viacheslav Sadykov: detector assembly, data analysis, and outreach.
Data and methods
I assembled Raspberry Pi-based detectors for the gLOWCOST global muon-monitoring network, then analysed multi-year muon-flux measurements against atmospheric data and solar and lunar ephemerides using Fourier transforms, coherence analysis and correlation tests to look for environmental and tidal modulation. Separately, I studied Forbush decreases by comparing gLOWCOST measurements with ACE MAG and SWEPAM solar-wind and magnetic-field data from the L1 point.
Recorded status and interpretation
Ongoing analysis; the detector-based outreach work was also part of what I presented at the Georgia State Capitol in February 2026.

Mapping the Magellanic Clouds

Research map ↑

How much of the Magellanic Clouds’ distance and structure can be recovered from public catalogs?

Recorded status
Complete
Publication state
Course talk and poster; not refereed research
Activity dates
Fall 2025 · ASTR 4200
Last research update
Date not recorded

A completed catalog-analysis project combines standard candles and proper-motion filtering to explore line-of-sight structure.

These outputs are coursework and a demonstration of catalog-level analysis.

Available work Poster (PDF) · Talk (PDF)

Question, contribution & methods +
The question
How accurately can the distances and line-of-sight structure of the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds be recovered from public survey catalogs alone?
Why it matters
The Clouds anchor the local distance ladder, and standard candles within them are the practical test of whether a period–luminosity calibration behaves.
My role
Sole author of the analysis, carried out for ASTR 4200 in Fall 2025.
Data and methods
OGLE variable-star catalogs combined with 2MASS near-infrared photometry and Gaia astrometry. Proper-motion filtering isolates genuine members; Cepheid and RR Lyrae period–luminosity relations then give distances, and the distribution of those distances traces the structure along the line of sight.
Recorded status and interpretation
Complete as a data-analysis project, presented as both a talk and a poster. This is coursework and a demonstration of catalog-level analysis, not a refereed research result.

Refereed publication

797 Montana

Research map ↑

What does a four-night light curve reveal about the rotation of a main-belt asteroid?

Recorded status
Published
Publication state
Minor Planet Bulletin 52(3), 207–208 (2025)
Activity dates
February 2025 observations · Spring 2025 course · 2025 publication
Last research update
Date not recorded

The published period is 4.545 ± 0.001 hr, with an amplitude of ΔV = 0.50 ± 0.03 mag, consistent with earlier period measurements.

Bentz, M. C., et al. (2025), “Broad-band Monitoring of 797 Montana.” My contribution was observing and photometric reduction.

Available work Full publication citation

Question, contribution & methods +
The question
What is the rotation period and light-curve amplitude of the main-belt asteroid 797 Montana, and do they agree with earlier measurements?
Why it matters
Comparing a new rotational light curve with earlier measurements checks the consistency of the asteroid’s measured period.
My role
Co-author. I took part in the observing campaign and the photometric reduction as part of the Spring 2025 astronomical techniques and instrumentation course at Georgia State University.
Data and methods
A four-night photometric campaign in February 2025 using the 24-inch Miller telescope at Hard Labor Creek Observatory, with CCD photometry reduced in IRAF and DS9.
Result
A best-fit rotation period of 4.545 ± 0.001 hr, consistent with previous measurements, with a variability amplitude of ΔV = 0.50 ± 0.03 mag. Published in the Minor Planet Bulletin.
