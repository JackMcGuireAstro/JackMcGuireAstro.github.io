How this is worked out

The CCD equation. S/N = S·t / √(S·t + npix·(Bsky·t + D·t + R²)). Here S is the star’s electrons per second inside the aperture and t is the exposure time. Bsky is the sky’s electrons per second per pixel, D is the dark current and R is the read noise. npix is the number of pixels in the aperture. The exposure time for a target S/N and the limiting magnitude are exact solutions of the same equation (both are quadratics), not approximations.

From magnitude to electrons. S = F0 × A × τ × 10−0.4(m + kX) × fap. F0 is the number of photons per second per cm² from a magnitude-0 star across the filter. For UBVRI it comes from the Vega fluxes of Bessell, Castelli & Plez (1998) times the filter width. For ugriz it comes from the AB definition (3631 Jy) with the filter widths of Fukugita et al. (1996). A is the mirror area minus the central obstruction. τ is the whole system’s throughput (optics, filter and detector quantum efficiency). k is the extinction per airmass and X is the airmass. fap is the fraction of a Gaussian star image inside the aperture: 1 − exp(−r²/2σ²), with σ = FWHM/2.355. That is 94% for a radius of one FWHM.

Sky and aperture. The sky’s electrons per pixel use the same F0, A and τ, with the sky brightness in mag/arcsec² times the pixel area. The sky is taken as given and does not get brighter with airmass. The dark, grey and bright presets are typical dark-site values (the CTIO table of Walker 1987 for UBVRI and Apache Point values for ugriz). Sites near towns, such as Red Buttes near Laramie, have brighter skies. npix = π r² / (plate scale)².

What is left out. This calculation leaves out scintillation, flat-field errors, digitisation noise, the bias level and the noise of the sky annulus. Very bright stars are limited by these effects, so treat S/N above a few hundred as an upper bound. The peak-pixel estimate assumes the star is centred on a pixel. It warns when that pixel passes 80% of the full well or of the 16-bit converter range (65,535 ADU).

How accurate is it? The equations are exact, but the answer is only as good as the inputs. Seeing, throughput and sky brightness change from night to night, so expect the S/N to be right within a few tens of percent, and the limiting magnitude within a few tenths. The instrumental zero point shown with the result is the magnitude that gives 1 e⁻/s above the atmosphere. Compare it with a zero point measured from real frames to calibrate the throughput.
