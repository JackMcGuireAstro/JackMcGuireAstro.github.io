Track one: things that flash

Supernovae, neutrino alerts, gravitational waves. CTAS collects public reports of anything in the sky that changed. These five lessons teach you to read one of those reports without believing more than it says.

Lesson 1 Things that flash ✓ done

A report is not a discovery

Every night, telescopes around the world compare tonight's sky to last night's and shout about the differences. CTAS listens to those shouts.

A transient is anything in the sky that changes on a human timescale: a star that explodes, a flash of gamma rays, a neutrino that arrives from a direction it shouldn't. When a survey spots one, it publishes a report: a position, a brightness, a time, and the name of whoever saw it.

Here's the habit this whole course is about. A report means someone said they saw something. It does not mean the thing is real, that anyone knows what it is, or that it's important. Most reports turn out to be boring. A few turn out to be the most interesting objects of the year. At the moment the report lands, you can't tell which.

CTAS never upgrades a report into a discovery. If a provider says "we saw a new source," CTAS records exactly that, with the provider's name attached, and nothing more.

The point is that you found the provider. On CTAS every record shows its source, and clicking through takes you to the original report on the provider's own site, not to a copy. When you cite a transient in real work, you cite that original, not CTAS.

Surveys report the most objects by far, but a single AstroNote from one astronomer can be the most useful thing on the page.

Lesson 2 Things that flash ✓ done

"We didn't see it" is real information

When a telescope points at a spot and finds nothing, that isn't a failure. It's a measurement with a number attached.

Call it an upper limit. It says: whatever is there, it's fainter than this. Upper limits are how astronomers learn when an explosion started (it wasn't there on Tuesday, it was there on Thursday) and how fast it's fading.

The trap is plotting a limit as if it were a detection. Then a rising supernova looks like it's flickering, and a fading one looks like it recovered. CTAS keeps limits on a separate line and draws them as arrows pointing down, meaning "the real value is somewhere below here."

AT2026zwn has both detections and limits in its record. Before you look: did anyone see it fade below their limit afterwards?

If the answer surprised you, notice that the surprise came from the arrows, not the dots. That's the skill.

Lesson 3 Things that flash ✓ done

Two sources, two answers, and that's fine

A classification is somebody's claim about what a thing is. Two experts can look at the same object and disagree. A catalog that quietly picks a winner is hiding something from you.

Neutrino alerts are the clearest example. IceCube reports a neutrino and a patch of sky it probably came from. Other teams then look at that patch and report what they find: sometimes a flaring galaxy, sometimes nothing. Their positions, sizes, and confidence levels don't always agree.

CTAS keeps every assertion side by side with the name of whoever asserted it. When two disagree, the record says so out loud. That's the whole design: the catalog would rather show you a conflict than pretend to resolve it.

For neutrino events, disagreements are usually about the first two: where the sky region is and whether a candidate counterpart is really associated. CTAS shows each provider's version and does not average them.

A "conflict" flag in CTAS isn't an error. It means the record is honest about what the community actually knows.

Lesson 4 Things that flash ✓ done

Taking it back doesn't make it disappear

Sometimes a report is wrong. A satellite glitch, a cosmic ray on the detector, a piece of space junk. The provider issues a retraction.

A bad catalog deletes the record. Now nobody can learn from the mistake, and anyone who built on it has no trail to follow. CTAS does the opposite: the retraction is added on top, the record's score drops to zero, and everything that came before stays visible.

There's a replay tool on each record that lets you scrub back in time and see what CTAS knew at any moment. For a retracted event, you can watch the score climb, then collapse when the retraction arrived.

Think of it like a lab notebook. You cross out the wrong line with a single stroke so it can still be read. You never tear out the page.

Retroactive scoring would erase the fact that people reasonably chased this. CTAS keeps the replay so the reasoning stays visible.

Lesson 5 Things that flash ✓ done

The score is a to-do list, not a truth meter

Every CTAS record has a number from 0 to 100. People want it to mean "how real" or "how important." It means neither.

The CTAS score answers one question: if you have a telescope free tonight, which records would benefit most from another look? It starts every record at 35 and moves it up or down for things like how recent the report is, how bright it was, whether anyone has taken a spectrum yet, and how many different kinds of messenger (light, neutrinos, gravitational waves) are involved.

The part that surprises people: having lots of data lowers the score. A well-studied supernova with three spectra doesn't need your telescope. A fresh, bright, unclassified blob does. So the highest scores go to the least-understood objects, on purpose.

Every record shows its exact score arithmetic, term by term. You can check the number yourself.

Existing spectra and a settled classification both subtract from the score. That doesn't make the supernova less interesting; it makes it less urgent. Scientific importance is your judgment to make. The score just tells you where a spare hour of telescope time does the most good.

Lesson 1 Other worlds ✓ done

You almost never see the planet

Nearly every known exoplanet was found by watching its star do something slightly odd. The planet itself stays invisible.

The two workhorse methods: a transit is a tiny, repeating dip in a star's brightness as the planet crosses in front of it. Radial velocity is a tiny, repeating wobble in the star's motion as the planet tugs it around. Transits tell you the planet's size. Wobbles tell you its mass. Get both and you know what it's made of.

Drag the slider to see how the dip depends on the planet's size. Notice how small an Earth is.

51 Peg b (1995) was the first planet found around a Sun-like star. Which method found it?

51 Peg b never transits from our point of view, so we still don't know its size, only a lower limit on its mass. That's typical: each method gives you part of the picture, and the catalogs record which part.

Its discoverers, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics for this measurement.

Lesson 2 Other worlds ✓ done

"Candidate" means the work isn't done

A survey like Kepler or TESS finds a dip and flags it. That's a candidate. It becomes confirmed only after someone rules out everything else that could make a dip.

And there are a lot of other things. A background eclipsing binary blended into the same pixel. A grazing stellar companion. An instrument hiccup with a suspiciously regular period. Roughly one in ten TESS candidates turns out to be one of these, so a candidate list is not a planet list.

There's a second wrinkle. The same planet can appear in several catalogs under slightly different names, and WorldsIndex won't merge them unless the evidence says they're the same object. So a famous planet can show up more than once, each entry carrying its own source. That's a feature: you can see exactly which catalog said what.

Lesson 3 Other worlds ✓ done

The habitable planet that wasn't there

In 2010 a team announced Gliese 581 g: a rocky world in the habitable zone of a nearby red dwarf, the first of its kind. It made headlines everywhere.

The signal was a small, slow wobble in the star. Four years later, another team showed that stars like Gliese 581 wobble on their own: magnetic spots rotate in and out of view and mimic a planet's tug. Once they corrected for the star's activity, the planet's signal vanished.

That makes Gliese 581 g refuted: not merely unconfirmed, but actively shown to be something else. Most catalogs simply removed it. WorldsIndex keeps it, labeled, with the sources that argued each side, because the refutation is itself a piece of science worth being able to find.

A refuted planet is the exoplanet version of a retracted transient. Same rule: cross it out, don't tear out the page.

The star was the impostor.

Stellar activity is the single biggest source of false radial-velocity planets, especially around small, active stars where the habitable zone sits close in. The lesson generalizes: the more exciting a claim, the more you should ask what else could produce the same signal.

The original team disputed the refutation for a while. The catalogs' consensus moved to "refuted" over several years, and the record keeps that history.

Lesson 4 Other worlds ✓ done

Two catalogs, two verdicts

Kepler-452 b was announced in 2015 as "Earth's cousin": similar size, similar orbit, similar star. NASA called it confirmed. Not everyone agreed.

Here's why. Kepler saw only a few transits, all of them faint. The planet was never "confirmed" by measuring its mass; it was statistically validated, meaning the team calculated that a false positive was very unlikely. In 2018 another group reran that calculation with a more careful model of Kepler's own noise and found the odds weren't good enough to call it confirmed. Real, probably. Confirmed, not by their standard.

So today one catalog lists Kepler-452 b as confirmed and another as controversial. Neither is wrong. They're using different rules, and WorldsIndex shows you both rather than choosing for you.

Fomalhaut b was photographed directly in 2008. It's now marked controversial. Why?

Open Fomalhaut b in WorldsIndex
It faded and it spread out.

Over a decade of Hubble images, the dot got fainter and larger. Planets don't do that; clouds of debris do. The leading explanation now is that Hubble caught the aftermath of two large icy bodies colliding. Some researchers still argue for a planet inside the cloud, which is why it's controversial rather than refuted.

Direct imaging finds few planets, but the ones it finds have a hard time hiding what they are, because you can watch them move.

Lesson 5 Other worlds ✓ done

Reading the plot everyone shows

Put orbital period on one axis and planet size on the other, both on log scales, and the whole known population lands on one chart. It has a shape, and the shape is mostly about our telescopes, not the universe.

Three things to look for. The bottom-right is empty: small planets on long orbits. They exist, we're sure of it, but their transits are too shallow and too rare for us to catch yet. The top-left is crowded: giant planets on orbits of a few days, so-called hot Jupiters. They're rare in reality, but they're the easiest possible thing to find, so the early surveys found them first. And there's a thin gap around one and a half to two Earth radii, which seems to be real: planets there tend to lose their atmospheres and shrink, or keep them and stay puffy.

What you can now do that most people can't

You can open a record of something in the sky and read it for exactly what it says. That sounds small. It's the skill that separates people who understand a headline from people who repeat it.

Field notes to keep

  1. A report is a claim with a name attached. Find the name.
  2. "Not seen" is a measurement. Read limits as "fainter than this."
  3. Two sources can disagree. A catalog that hides that is hiding something from you.
  4. Retractions and refutations are science. Keep them, labeled.
  5. A priority score is a queue, not a truth meter.
  6. Ask what else could make this signal. The star usually can.
  7. The shape of a population plot is mostly the shape of our instruments.
